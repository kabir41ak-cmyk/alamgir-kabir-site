Participation is a right: disability inclusion must begin with listening

By Md. Alamgir Kabir | Bangladesh

A person can be invited to a meeting and still have no influence over what is decided. A workplace can say it welcomes people with disabilities, while its recruitment process remains inaccessible. A health service can be available, but the information about it may be difficult to understand or reach.

These are ordinary situations. They are also where disability inclusion is tested.

I was born without a right hand. My childhood was shaped by financial hardship, too. After my father was injured, I started selling newspapers to help support my family. There were times when education and other opportunities had to take a back seat to the needs of the household. I learned early that a person’s circumstances can affect the choices available to them.

My involvement in child rights began through grassroots volunteering. I worked with other young people on awareness activities related to child marriage, child labour and violence against children. Later, I became involved in youth initiatives and community response work, including activities during the COVID-19 emergency and floods.

Over time, my work brought me into the humanitarian sector in Cox’s Bazar. I support health operations serving refugee and host communities, including registration and information processes. The work has made me pay attention to details that can easily be overlooked. Does someone understand where they need to go? Can they get the information they need? Do they know what happens after a referral? Can they ask a question and receive a clear answer?

A service may be available, but that does not automatically mean everyone can use it.

I know something about this from my own experience. Working with one hand can make certain tasks difficult, particularly when a process assumes that everyone will complete them in the same way. Sometimes a small change in a tool, arrangement or working method can make a real difference. The conversation should be about what is needed to do the job properly, not about whether a person deserves an opportunity.

Reasonable accommodation should be treated as a normal part of a fair workplace. It should not depend on someone having to repeatedly explain or defend their disability.

Disability is one part of a person’s life. It does not tell us everything about their circumstances. Gender, age, income, location and social background also affect the opportunities people have. These factors often overlap.

Consider a girl with a disability living in a remote community. Distance may make healthcare difficult to reach. Her family’s financial situation may limit her choices. Gender expectations may affect whether she can travel, continue her education or speak freely about her needs. Looking at disability alone will not explain all the barriers she faces.

The same is true in humanitarian settings. A child with a disability in a displacement-affected community may have difficulty receiving information, reaching a service or taking part in decisions about their own protection. Organisations need to understand these different experiences when they plan and deliver programmes.

This is where listening matters. Data can help identify who is being missed, but figures cannot tell the whole story. Organisations need to speak with people who use their services, listen to their concerns and be prepared to change how things are done.

Accessibility is often associated with ramps and buildings. Those are important, but the issue is much wider. It includes transport, websites, application forms, public information, communication, workplaces and the way a service is organised. An accessible building is of little help if a person cannot understand the information inside it. A job announcement that welcomes applicants with disabilities means little if the application process creates barriers.

In humanitarian work, accessibility needs to be considered from the beginning. It belongs in registration, health services, referrals, protection, safeguarding, community engagement and information management. Persons with disabilities and their representative organisations should help identify problems and shape the response. They should not be consulted only after a programme has already been designed.

I have taken part in youth and community initiatives where people were invited to share their views. I value those opportunities. But I have come to think that there is another question worth asking after every consultation: what happened to the suggestions people made?

Were participants able to influence the agenda? Could they disagree with the organisers? Did anyone explain which recommendations were accepted and which were not? Being present in a room is not the same as having a voice in the decision.

This matters for persons with disabilities, but it also matters for children, young people and other groups whose experiences are often left out. Participation must be safe and meaningful. No one should be expected to speak on behalf of every person who shares their identity or experience.

In Bangladesh, disability inclusion also needs to be part of conversations about climate change and disaster preparedness. My experience in community response work during floods has made one practical issue clear to me: warnings and assistance are useful only when people can access them. Early-warning messages need to reach people in formats they can understand. Evacuation plans and shelters need to consider accessibility. Relief registration and distribution should not leave people dependent on chance or on whether a neighbour happens to be available to help.

The International Day of Persons with Disabilities on 3 December 2026 comes in the year the United Nations marks 20 years since the adoption of the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities. The theme, “The CRPD at 20: Building on Progress, Advancing the Future”, gives us a reason to look at what has changed and, just as importantly, what remains difficult in everyday life.

I do not see my disability as the only defining part of who I am. My perspective also comes from working with children and young people, community initiatives and humanitarian programmes. I do not seek sympathy. I believe in empathy, dignity and equal participation.

For organisations, the next steps are practical. Make information accessible. Review recruitment and workplace arrangements. Ask people what prevents them from using a service. Involve persons with disabilities in decisions from the start, and tell them what happened to the feedback they gave.

These steps may sound simple, but they require organisations to share influence and be willing to change familiar ways of working.

The real measure of inclusion is not how often we speak about it. It is whether people can access opportunities, express their views and influence decisions that affect their lives. That should be possible on 3 December, and on every other day of the year.

Participation is a right.
